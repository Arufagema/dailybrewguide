How to Dial In a Baratza Encore for Pour-Over Without Guessing

2026-10-15

Short answer: On a standard Baratza Encore, start at setting 15 for a single-cup V60 or Kalita and 18–20 for a Chemex or a larger batch. Brew 15 g of coffee with 250 g of water and time the brew. Then change the grind two numbers at a time, based on how long the brew took and how it tasted. Most people get it right within three or four brews if they keep everything else the same.

The rest of this guide explains how to make those three or four brews count.

Why the Number Alone Won't Save You

The Encore has 40 settings, with 1 the finest and 40 the coarsest. Two Encores set to "15" can still grind a bit differently. Burr wear, factory calibration, and roast level all move the target. A light Ethiopian may want 13, and a dark Brazilian may be happy at 18.

So use the starting number as a starting point. What actually gets you to a good cup is a repeatable test, done the same way each time.

A note on the Encore ESP

If you have the newer Encore ESP, its scale works differently. Settings 1–20 are fine steps for espresso, and 21–40 are larger steps for everything else. Pour-over usually lands somewhere in the mid-to-high 20s. Start around 26 and use the same method below, but move one number at a time, because each step on that half of the dial is bigger.

Step 1: Lock Down Everything Except the Grind

You can only diagnose grind size if nothing else changes between brews. Pick a recipe and keep it fixed for the whole session:

  • Dose: 15 g coffee
  • Water: 250 g (about a 1:16.7 ratio)
  • Water temperature: just off the boil, roughly 93–96°C (200–205°F)
  • Pours: a 40–45 g bloom for 30–45 seconds, then two or three even pours to 250 g
  • Target total time (V60/Kalita, single cup): 2:45–3:30

Without a scale you're still guessing. A coffee scale with timer is the most useful thing you can add to a pour-over setup, more useful than a better grinder. It lets you measure dose, water, and time on one device, so you'll know what changed when a cup tastes different.

A gooseneck kettle also helps a lot. You don't need it to make good coffee. But an even, controlled pour means your drawdown time reflects your grind and not a splashy pour that churned up the bed.

Step 2: Brew, Then Read Two Signals

After each brew, write down two things: total time and taste. Time gives you a quick, objective check. Taste gives the final verdict.

Reading the time

  • Finished under 2:30: The grind is probably too coarse. Water ran straight through.
  • 2:45–3:30: You're in range. Now judge by taste.
  • Over 4:00: The grind is probably too fine, or fines are clogging the filter.

Reading the taste

What you taste What it usually means What to do
Sour, sharp, thin, salty, vanishing finish Under-extracted Go finer by 2
Bitter, harsh, drying, hollow, lingering ashy finish Over-extracted Go coarser by 2
Sweet, balanced, clear, pleasant aftertaste Dialed in Stop. Write the number down.
Sour and bitter at once Uneven extraction Fix your pour technique before touching the grind

The last row catches a lot of people. If a cup is sour and bitter at the same time, the grind usually isn't the problem. Some of the coffee extracted too much and some too little, usually because water channeled through one side of the bed. Swirl the brewer gently after your final pour so the bed settles flat. Try to pour in the center, not down the paper walls.

Step 3: Adjust in Twos, Then Fine-Tune in Ones

Moving one notch at a time on a standard Encore makes changes too small to taste reliably. Moving two notches gives you a difference you can actually notice. A good sequence looks like this:

  1. Brew at 15. Note the time and taste.
  2. Go to 13 or 17, depending on what you tasted.
  3. If that cup was better, try one more step of 2 in the same direction. If it overshot (sour became bitter, or the reverse), the best setting is between your two attempts.
  4. Split the difference with a single-number change.

By the fourth brew you've usually tried enough settings to tell which one is right. Write it on a piece of tape on the grinder, along with the coffee's name. You'll want it the next time you buy that bean.

Small Habits That Make the Encore Behave

The Encore is a great entry grinder. It does produce more fines (very small particles) than high-end burrs, though, and these habits keep it consistent.

Purge after big changes. If you move the dial more than a few numbers, grind and throw away 2–3 g first. A little old coffee stays in the chute.

Adjust going finer while the motor runs. Turning toward finer with beans sitting between stationary burrs can strain the mechanism. Turning while the motor runs is gentler.

Tame static with a drop of water. Before grinding, stir a single drop of water into the beans with the back of a spoon. The grounds will cling much less to the chute and bin.

Clean it every few weeks. Oils and fines build up around the burrs and slowly shift your settings. Remove the hopper, twist out the top burr, and brush it out. A stiff grinder cleaning brush does this in two minutes. Don't use water on the burrs.

Check the low end if things seem off. If setting 15 tastes wildly over- or under-extracted with several different coffees, your grinder may be out of calibration. Baratza has a free calibration guide on its support site. It covers resetting the burr ring so the burrs just touch at the bottom of the scale.

When You've Hit the Encore's Ceiling

Sometimes you'll find a setting where the coffee isn't sour, but going one notch finer makes it bitter and slow to drain. That's often the fines catching up with you. Before blaming the grinder, try this:

  • Keep the finer setting but use slightly cooler water (around 90°C) or fewer pours.
  • Use a flat-bottom brewer like a Kalita Wave, which handles fines more forgivingly than a cone.

If you've done all of that and your light roasts still taste muddy, you may have outgrown the Encore. Most people never get there, though. A well-maintained Encore with a consistent recipe makes excellent everyday pour-over.

FAQ

Does the grind setting change when I switch to a new bag of beans? Usually, yes, by a little. Different origins, processing methods, and roast levels extract differently. Start from your last good setting, brew once, and adjust by 1–2 numbers based on taste. You won't need to start from scratch.

Should I grind finer for a smaller dose, like 12 g for a small cup? Often, yes. A smaller dose makes a shallower coffee bed, so water drains faster. Going one or two numbers finer usually brings the brew time back into range. For a larger dose, like 30 g in a Chemex, go coarser.

My Encore is a few years old. Do the burrs need replacing? The Encore's burrs normally last several years of daily home use. If you've cleaned and calibrated the grinder and still find you need much finer settings than you used to for the same coffee, or the grinds look noticeably uneven, the burrs may be worn. Replacement burrs are inexpensive and easy to swap in yourself.
