Best Pour-Over Dripper for One Cup When You're Short on Counter Space

2026-10-17

The short answer

For one cup in a small kitchen, get a small cone dripper (size 01) that sits directly on your mug. A plastic one costs very little, won't break, weighs almost nothing, and stores upside-down inside a mug in your cupboard. That way it takes up no counter space at all. You don't need a carafe, a stand or a brewing station, and you have one fewer thing to wash.

If even a small cone feels like too much to store, a collapsible silicone dripper folds nearly flat and fits in a drawer. You give up a little consistency, but you save a lot of space.

The rest of this guide covers why those two picks work, what to look for, and a reliable single-cup recipe.

Why a small cone suits small kitchens

It uses the mug you already own

The setup that eats counter space is the full kit: dripper, glass server, stand, scale and kettle all sitting out. Brewing one cup straight into your mug removes the server completely. Most size-01 cones have a base about 10–11 cm wide, which fits a normal mug. If your mug has a wide mouth, check that the dripper's base is wider than the rim before you buy.

Size 01 is the right size for one cup

Dripper sizes roughly follow how much coffee they handle:

  • Size 01: about 1–2 cups (up to roughly 20 g coffee)
  • Size 02: about 1–4 cups

A size 02 can make a single cup, but your small dose of grounds sits in a shallow bed spread across a wide cone, and that can make the brew run fast and taste thin. The 01 keeps a deeper coffee bed for 12–18 g doses, so extraction is easier to control. It's also smaller to store.

For a cramped kitchen, plastic is the practical winner. It's the material people tend to keep using, because nothing bad happens when you knock it into the sink. A size 01 plastic cone dripper is the one I'd buy first.

Cone, flat-bottom, or immersion?

All three are good. They behave differently, though, and some forgive mistakes better than others.

Cone drippers (one large hole)

A single large hole lets water drain quickly, so your grind size and pouring speed control the brew. That gives you lots of control and bright, clear cups. It also means you'll have some uneven brews while you learn. Choose this if you enjoy tinkering.

Flat-bottom drippers (several small holes)

A flat bed and small drain holes slow the flow down, so the brew is less sensitive to sloppy pouring. Cups usually taste a little rounder and sweeter. The catch is that many flat-bottom models need their own proprietary filters, which means one more box to store and one more thing to run out of.

Immersion-style drippers (with a valve)

These let the coffee steep like a French press and then drain through a paper filter when you set the dripper on your mug. They are the most forgiving option, and you don't need a gooseneck kettle. The downside is size: they're usually bigger than a small cone and less pleasant to store. If you hate the idea of owning a special kettle, though, this saves space overall.

My take: if you already have a gooseneck kettle or are happy to get a compact one, go with the cone. If you'd like to pour from any old kettle, go with immersion.

Small-space accessories that are worth it

Don't buy everything at once. Here's what really matters:

  1. A scale. This matters more than which dripper you choose. Measuring by eye is the most common reason home pour-over tastes different every day. A slim coffee scale with timer slides into a drawer and handles both weight and timing.
  2. A small gooseneck kettle (optional for cones). A 600 ml–0.8 L model is plenty for one cup, and its footprint is about the size of a large mug. A small gooseneck kettle 600ml gives you far more control than a regular spout. Skip it if you go with an immersion dripper.
  3. Filters that fit. Size 01 cone filters are cheap and easy to find, and the flat pack slides behind other things in a cupboard.

You can store the whole kit in about one shoebox: dripper nested in your mug, filters tucked beside it, scale underneath.

A reliable one-cup recipe (cone dripper)

This makes about 220–230 ml in the mug.

  • Coffee: 15 g, ground medium-fine (a little finer than table salt)
  • Water: 250 g at 93–96 °C (just off the boil, rested about 30 seconds)
  • Ratio: 1:16.7
  • Target total time: 2:30–3:00

Steps:

  1. Put a filter in the dripper and rinse it with hot water. This removes the papery taste and warms your mug. Pour the rinse water out.
  2. Add the coffee, shake it level, set everything on the scale and tare it to zero.
  3. Bloom: pour 40 g of water, enough to wet all the grounds. Wait 30–45 seconds. Fresh coffee will bubble as gas escapes.
  4. Pour slowly in small circles up to 150 g by about 1:15. Keep the stream off the paper walls.
  5. Pour the rest up to 250 g by about 1:45.
  6. Give the dripper a gentle swirl to flatten the bed, then let it drain.

Adjusting the taste

  • Sour, thin, or finished in under 2:15? Grind finer.
  • Bitter, dry, or still dripping past 3:30? Grind coarser.
  • Too strong or too weak, but balanced? Keep the grind and change the ratio. Try 15 g to 230 g for stronger coffee or 15 g to 270 g for lighter coffee.

Change only one thing at a time. That way you'll know which change made the difference.

If you go collapsible

A collapsible silicone pour over dripper makes sense for tiny studios, dorm rooms, or anyone who also wants a travel brewer. Because the soft walls don't hold the filter as firmly, use the same recipe but pour more gently and grind slightly finer to make up for faster, less even flow. Make sure it's fully popped open and sitting firmly on the mug before you pour. Hot water and a wobbly dripper are a bad combination.

FAQ

Can I use a size 02 dripper for one cup? Yes, especially with doses of 18 g and up. With 12–15 g, the bed gets shallow and tends to drain fast, so grind a bit finer to compensate. If you only ever make one cup, a size 01 is easier to get right and smaller to store.

Do I really need a gooseneck kettle? For a cone dripper, it helps a lot, because steady, slow pouring is most of the technique. You can manage with a regular kettle by pouring in short, careful bursts, but expect less consistent results. If you don't want a gooseneck at all, an immersion-style dripper avoids the problem.

Plastic dripper: is it safe and does it affect taste? Most plastic drippers sold for coffee are made from heat-resistant, food-safe plastics meant for near-boiling water. Check the product listing for the material and heat rating. Taste-wise, plastic holds very little heat, so it actually keeps brew temperature steady more easily than an unheated ceramic dripper. Wash it with mild soap, and an occasional soak removes coffee oil stains.
