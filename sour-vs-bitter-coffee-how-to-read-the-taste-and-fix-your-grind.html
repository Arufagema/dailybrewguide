Sour vs Bitter Coffee: How to Read the Taste and Fix Your Grind

2026-10-16

Short version: sour coffee is usually under-extracted, so grind finer. Bitter coffee is usually over-extracted, so grind coarser. Change one thing at a time, in small steps, and taste after each change. Most cups get noticeably better within two or three brews.

There's a bit more to it than that, though. Sour and bitter get mixed up a lot, and grind size isn't the only cause. Here's how to tell what you're tasting and what to change.

What Extraction Actually Means

When hot water meets ground coffee, it dissolves compounds in a rough order:

  1. Acids and fruity aromatics come out first. They're bright, tangy and sometimes sharp.
  2. Sugars and the heavier browned flavors come next. These give sweetness, body and caramel or chocolate notes.
  3. Bitter, harsh compounds come out last. A little adds depth. Too much tastes like burnt toast or aspirin.

A good cup stops somewhere in the middle. If you stop too early, acid dominates and the sweetness never arrives. If you go too long, the bitter tail takes over. Grind size is the strongest control you have, because finer grounds expose more surface area and slow the water down. Both of those increase extraction.

How to Tell Sour From Bitter

This is harder than it sounds. Many people call any unpleasant coffee "bitter." Pay attention to where and how the flavor hits.

Signs your coffee is sour (under-extracted)

  • A sharp, tangy taste at the front and sides of your tongue, like a squeeze of lemon or an unripe plum
  • It tastes thin, watery or hollow, even at a normal strength
  • The finish is short. The flavor disappears almost as soon as you swallow
  • Saltiness is sometimes a sign too

Signs your coffee is bitter (over-extracted)

  • A heavy, lingering taste at the back of your tongue
  • It tastes harsh, ashy or medicinal
  • A dry, puckering feeling, like strong black tea that steeped too long. That's astringency, and it often comes with over-extraction
  • The finish is long and unpleasant

Sour vs. bright acidity

Pleasant acidity is not the same as sourness. A light-roasted Ethiopian coffee should taste a bit like berries or citrus. The difference is balance. Good acidity comes with sweetness and feels juicy. Sourness feels sharp and empty, with nothing behind it.

A quick diagnostic trick

Let the cup cool to warm. Cooling makes flaws stand out. Sourness gets more obvious as the coffee cools, and so does the dry grip of over-extraction. If you still can't decide, deliberately brew one cup a few steps finer and one a few steps coarser. Tasting the extremes side by side will train your palate faster than anything else.

How to Fix Your Grind

The process

  1. Keep everything else the same: the same dose, water amount, temperature and method.
  2. Move the grind in small steps. On most home burr grinders, that's 1–2 clicks or settings at a time. On espresso, steps should be even smaller.
  3. Brew, taste, write it down. A note on your phone works fine. Record grind setting, dose, water and total brew time.
  4. Overshoot on purpose if you're stuck. If you go too far and the cup turns bitter, you know the sweet spot is between your last two settings.

A consistent grinder matters more here than almost any other gear. Blade grinders produce a mix of dust and boulders, so the same cup can be sour and bitter at once. Then grind changes won't fix anything. If you're serious about dialing in, a burr grinder for pour over is the upgrade that pays off most.

Starting points by method

Use these as a baseline, then adjust by taste:

Method Ratio (coffee:water) Water temp Target total time
Pour-over (V60, Kalita), 1 cup 1:15–1:17 92–96°C (198–205°F) 2:30–3:30
Chemex 1:15–1:17 93–96°C 3:30–4:30
French press 1:14–1:16 93–96°C 4:00 steep, then plunge
AeroPress 1:12–1:16 80–92°C 1:30–2:30
Espresso 1:2 (e.g. 18 g in → 36 g out) 90–94°C 25–32 seconds

For pour-over and espresso, time is your clue. If a V60 drains in under two minutes and tastes sour, the grind is almost certainly too coarse. If it stalls past four minutes and tastes bitter, go coarser.

To hit these numbers reliably, you need to measure. A coffee scale with timer lets you weigh the dose and water and watch the clock in one place. Guessing with scoops can easily put you off by 20–30% from one day to the next.

When It's Not the Grind

If you've worked through the grind and the cup still tastes wrong, check these:

  • Water temperature. Water well below 90°C under-extracts, especially with light roasts. If your kettle doesn't show temperature, letting boiling water sit for 30 seconds gets you close. A gooseneck kettle with temperature control makes this exact and also helps you pour evenly.
  • Ratio. Using too much coffee for the water can taste sour and heavy at the same time. Using too little tastes thin and can tip toward bitter. Stay within the ranges above.
  • Roast level. Dark roasts extract easily and carry roasty bitterness of their own. Brew them coarser and cooler (around 88–92°C). Light roasts are dense and resist extraction. Grind finer and use hotter water, up to 96°C or even just off the boil.
  • Uneven extraction. In espresso, water can punch a channel through the coffee puck. You get sour and bitter in the same shot, often with a fast, spurting pour. Fix that with better distribution and level tamping before you touch the grind. In pour-over, pouring the same spot over and over can cause similar problems.
  • Stale beans. Coffee more than about 4–6 weeks past its roast date tastes flat and papery, and grind changes can't bring it back.
  • Water quality. Very soft or distilled water tends to taste flat and sour. Very hard water can mute flavor. Filtered tap water is a good middle ground for most people.

A Simple Rule to Remember

Sour → finer, hotter or longer. Bitter → coarser, cooler or shorter.

Start with the grind, because it has the biggest effect. Move in small steps, and trust your tongue over any chart, including the one above. The numbers get you close. Your taste decides when you're done.

FAQ

Can coffee be sour and bitter at the same time?

Yes. It usually means uneven extraction. Some particles are under-extracted while others are over-extracted. Common causes are an inconsistent grinder (especially a blade grinder), channeling in espresso, or uneven pouring. Fix the consistency first. Changing grind size alone won't solve it.

How many clicks should I adjust my grinder?

Start with 1–2 steps per brew for pour-over and French press. For espresso, use the smallest adjustment your grinder allows. Grinders differ a lot, so "two clicks" on one model can be a bigger change than five on another. Small, recorded changes are what matter.

Why does my dark roast taste bitter no matter what I do?

Some bitterness is part of the roast itself, not a brewing mistake. Try grinding a bit coarser, dropping the water temperature to 88–90°C, and shortening the brew time. If it's still too harsh, that coffee may just be roasted darker than you enjoy. A medium roast is worth trying.
