Chemex vs V60 for Clean, Tea-Like Coffee at Home

2026-10-19

Short answer: If you want the cleanest, most tea-like cup with the least effort, get a Chemex. Its thick paper filters hold back almost all oils and fine particles, so the coffee comes out bright and very clear almost by default. Choose a V60 if you want a clean cup that still has a bit more body and sweetness, and you're happy to learn some technique. The V60 rewards practice and gives you more control. The Chemex is more forgiving and makes a larger batch more easily.

The rest of this guide explains why the two cups taste different and gives a recipe for each.

Why They Taste Different

The two brewers have a similar cone shape. The difference in the cup comes mostly from the filter paper and how fast water drains through it.

The filter is doing most of the work

Chemex filters are much thicker than standard cone filters. Thicker paper traps more coffee oils and more of the tiny particles called "fines" that cloud a cup. That's why Chemex coffee often looks almost translucent and tastes light-bodied, crisp and delicate. It's what people mean by "tea-like."

V60 filters are thinner, so a little more oil and a few more fines get into the cup. The coffee is still clean, but it has a bit more texture and roundness.

Flow rate and control

The V60 has one large hole at the bottom and spiral ribs on the inside wall. The ribs let air escape and keep the paper from sealing against the cone, so water drains quickly. As a result, your pouring controls the brew: how fast, how high and where you pour all change the extraction.

The Chemex's thick paper slows the drain on its own, and you have less to manage. One quirk: the wet filter can stick to the smooth glass wall and block airflow, which slows the drain. The folded filter has a three-layer side, and placing that side over the pour spout keeps an air channel open.

Batch size

  • V60 (size 02): works best from 1 to 2 cups (about 250–500 g of water).
  • Chemex (6-cup): works best from 2 to 4 cups (about 500–800 g). A single cup in a Chemex can stall or extract unevenly because the coffee bed is too shallow for the thick paper.

Whichever you choose, a pour-over kettle makes a big difference, especially with the V60. A gooseneck kettle lets you pour slowly and exactly where you aim, which matters more than which cone you use.

Getting the Most Tea-Like Cup: Shared Principles

These apply to both brewers:

  1. Choose light or light-medium roasts. Washed Ethiopian, Kenyan and many Central American coffees naturally taste floral, citrusy and tea-like. Dark roasts won't taste that way regardless of brewer.
  2. Use hot water. For light roasts, aim for 94–100°C (201–212°F). Water just off the boil is fine. Cooler water tends to under-extract light roasts and makes them taste sour and thin, which is not the same as delicate.
  3. Grind evenly. Clarity depends on an even grind. A blade grinder produces dust and boulders, and that muddies any cup. A decent burr coffee grinder is the most useful upgrade if clean coffee is your goal.
  4. Rinse the paper. Pour hot water through the empty filter before brewing to remove any papery taste and preheat the brewer. Discard the rinse water.
  5. Weigh everything. A coffee scale with timer removes the guesswork. Measuring by scoops is the most common reason pour-over results vary from day to day.

V60 Recipe (1 cup)

  • Coffee: 15 g
  • Water: 250 g (ratio about 1:16.7)
  • Grind: medium-fine, roughly like table salt
  • Total time: 2:45–3:30

Steps:

  1. Rinse the filter, discard the water, then add the coffee and shake the cone gently to level the bed.
  2. Bloom: Start the timer and pour 40–45 g of water, making sure all the grounds are wet. Swirl the cone gently and wait until 0:45.
  3. Pour slowly in small circles up to 150 g by about 1:15, avoiding the paper walls.
  4. Pour to 250 g by about 1:45.
  5. Give the brewer one gentle swirl to flatten the bed and let it drain. It should finish between 2:45 and 3:30.

Troubleshooting: If it drains in under 2:30 and tastes sour or hollow, grind finer. If it takes longer than about 3:45 and tastes bitter or drying, grind coarser. For an even cleaner V60, pour more gently and from lower down. Less agitation keeps fines from migrating and clogging the filter.

Chemex Recipe (2–3 cups)

  • Coffee: 32 g
  • Water: 520 g (ratio about 1:16)
  • Grind: medium-coarse, between table salt and kosher salt
  • Total time: 4:00–5:00

Steps:

  1. Unfold the filter with the three-layer side toward the spout. Rinse it thoroughly, then pour the rinse water out through the spout while holding the filter in place.
  2. Add the coffee and level the bed.
  3. Bloom: Pour 70–80 g of water, wet all the grounds, and wait 45 seconds.
  4. Pour to 300 g in slow spirals by about 1:30.
  5. Let the level drop by an inch or so, then pour to 520 g by about 2:30.
  6. Let it drain. Lift out the filter, swirl the carafe to mix, and serve.

Troubleshooting: If the drawdown passes 5:30 and the coffee tastes harsh, grind coarser and check that the filter isn't sealed against the spout. If it runs fast and tastes weak, grind a little finer. Chemex grind settings are coarser than most people expect, so start coarser than you would for a V60.

The Honest Tradeoffs

Chemex downsides: The filters cost more and are harder to find in ordinary shops. The glass carafe is fragile and has a narrow neck that's awkward to clean (a bottle brush helps). Single cups are mediocre. Some people find the body too thin, especially with medium roasts.

V60 downsides: It's sensitive to technique, so your first week of cups will vary. Small changes to grind or pouring show up clearly in the taste. You also need a separate mug or server underneath.

Common to both: Neither will hide stale coffee or a poor grinder. Fresh beans (roughly 1–5 weeks past the roast date) and an even grind matter more than which brewer you choose.

If you can't decide, a practical approach is to buy a V60 first. It's inexpensive, and learning to use it teaches you what grind size, pouring speed and water temperature do to the cup. If you later find yourself chasing more clarity or brewing for a household, add a Chemex. Many home brewers keep both.

FAQ

Can I use Chemex filters in a V60, or V60 filters in a Chemex? Not well. The shapes and sizes differ. A V60 filter is far too small for a Chemex, and a Chemex filter doesn't seat properly in a V60. If you want a V60 cup with more clarity, try a different V60-shaped paper or reduce agitation instead of improvising.

Is a Chemex just a bigger V60? No. They're both cones, but the Chemex's thick paper and smooth glass walls slow the drain and remove more oils. That produces a lighter, clearer cup, and it needs a coarser grind and longer brew time.

Why does my "tea-like" coffee just taste weak or sour? It's usually under-extracted. Try a finer grind, hotter water (close to boiling for light roasts), or a slightly stronger ratio such as 1:15. Tea-like coffee should still taste sweet and complete, just light-bodied and clear.
