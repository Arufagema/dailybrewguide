Best Burr Grinder Under $100 for French Press Beginners

The short answer: For French press on a budget, get a manual (hand) burr grinder in the $40–$80 range before you consider an electric one. A good hand grinder at that price gives you more consistent, coarser grounds than almost any electric grinder under $100 — and French press doesn't need many beans per batch, so cranking takes under a minute.

If you'll grind for more than two people every morning and the hand-cranking wears on you, step up to a budget electric conical burr grinder instead — just know you're trading a little grind quality for convenience at this price.

Below is why, what to actually look for, and how to dial it in.

Why burr, and why not a blade grinder

A blade grinder chops beans randomly. You get powder and boulders in the same batch. In a French press — which steeps grounds directly in water and uses a mesh filter — that powder slips through the screen, makes your coffee muddy, and keeps extracting after you press, turning the last few sips bitter.

A burr grinder crushes beans between two abrasive surfaces set a fixed distance apart. That gives you a far more uniform particle size, which is the single biggest lever for better French press coffee. Uniform coarse grounds mean a clean, full-bodied cup and a plunger that pushes down smoothly instead of fighting you.

This is the one place I'd tell a beginner not to cheap out with a blade grinder "for now." The grinder matters more than the press itself.

Why manual wins under $100 for French press specifically

The honest reason is cost per quality. Burrs are the expensive part. In a $60 hand grinder, nearly your whole budget goes to the burrs and a simple mechanism. In a $60 electric grinder, a chunk of the budget goes to the motor, housing, and hopper — so the burrs are smaller and coarser-tolerance.

French press also plays to a hand grinder's strengths:

  • Small batches. A single French press mug uses roughly 25–30g of beans. That's about 40–60 seconds of cranking. For a 3–4 cup press (around 50–60g), it's a couple of minutes. Manageable, not miserable.
  • Coarse setting. Coarse grinds are the fastest and easiest grind for a hand grinder — you're taking bigger bites, so there's less to crank through than for espresso.
  • No static, no mess. Manual grinders rarely spray grounds everywhere the way cheap electrics do.

The tradeoff is real: hand grinding is a daily effort, and grinding for a crowd gets old fast. If that's you, the budget electric is the right call despite slightly less uniform grounds.

What to look for at this price

Conical steel burrs

At every price under $100, look for stainless steel conical burrs. Avoid ceramic-only budget burrs if you can — they're fine but chip-prone and often slower. Steel conical burrs hold a coarse setting well, which is exactly what French press needs.

An external, clicky adjustment

You want a grinder where you can set the coarseness and return to it reliably. Many hand grinders adjust by a numbered dial or an audible "click" system. More clicks = finer. For French press you'll live near the coarse end of the range. A grinder with clear, repeatable settings saves you from re-dialing every morning.

Capacity that matches your press

Hand grinders typically hold 15–35g in the catch cup. If you brew a big press, check that it holds enough for one batch so you're not grinding in two rounds.

A stable grip or non-slip base

Cheap grinders that wobble are genuinely annoying. A rubberized base (electric) or a silicone sleeve / forearm-brace shape (manual) makes the job easier.

How coarse is "coarse" for French press?

Aim for grounds that look like coarse sea salt or raw sugar — distinct, gritty particles, not powder. If you rub them between your fingers, you should feel individual grains.

A practical starting point on most hand grinders is around 25–30 clicks from zero (zero = burrs just touching), but every grinder's scale differs, so treat that as a ballpark and adjust by taste.

Dialing in by cup, not by number:

  • Muddy, silty, over-bitter, hard to plunge? Grind coarser.
  • Weak, sour, watery even after a full steep? Grind finer.

Change one or two clicks at a time. You'll usually find your spot within three or four brews.

A reliable French press recipe to pair with it

Once your grinder is sorted, this ratio is a dependable starting point:

  1. Use a 1:15 ratio — e.g., 30g coffee to 450g (450ml) water. A kitchen scale helps enormously here. coffee scale with timer
  2. Heat water to about 200°F (93°C) — just off the boil. If you don't have a thermometer, boil, then wait 30–45 seconds.
  3. Add grounds, pour all the water in, and start a timer.
  4. At about 1 minute, stir the crust gently and give it a quick skim of the foam if you like a cleaner cup.
  5. Let it steep until 4 minutes total, then press slowly and steadily.
  6. Pour it all out immediately into mugs or a carafe. Coffee left sitting on the grounds keeps extracting and turns bitter.

FAQ

Can't I just buy pre-ground coffee and skip the grinder? You can, and it's better than a blade grinder. But pre-ground coffee is usually ground for drip, which is too fine for French press — expect a muddier cup and more sediment. It also goes stale within days of grinding. A burr grinder you use right before brewing is the biggest flavor upgrade for the money.

How long does hand grinding actually take for French press? For a single mug (25–30g), roughly 40–60 seconds on a coarse setting. For a full 3–4 cup press, about 1.5–2.5 minutes depending on the grinder and your beans. Coarse is the quickest grind, so French press is the least demanding use case for a hand grinder.

Is ceramic or steel better for burrs? For French press at this budget, steel conical burrs are the easier recommendation — they hold a coarse setting well, grind faster, and resist chipping. Ceramic burrs aren't bad and stay cool, but cheap ceramic ones are more fragile and often slower. Either works; just prioritize conical and consistent settings over the burr material.
