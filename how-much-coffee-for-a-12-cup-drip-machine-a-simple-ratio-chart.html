How Much Coffee for a 12-Cup Drip Machine: A Simple Ratio Chart

2026-10-20

The short answer: for a full 12-cup drip machine, use about 105–115 grams of coffee, which is roughly 21–23 level tablespoons or 11 standard coffee scoops. That works out to about 1:16 coffee to water by weight, a good starting point for most people. If you like it bolder, go up to about 120 g. If you like it milder, drop to about 100 g.

The rest of this guide covers why those numbers work, how to scale them for a partial pot, and how to adjust when the coffee isn't quite right.

First, What Is a "Cup" on Your Machine?

A "cup" on a drip coffee maker isn't a measuring cup and isn't a mug. Most American drip machines count a cup as 5 fluid ounces (about 148 ml), so a "12-cup" pot holds roughly 60 ounces, or about 1.8 liters. Some brands use 6 ounces per cup, which makes a full pot closer to 72 ounces.

To check yours, fill the reservoir to the 12 line with a measuring cup, or look in the manual. This guide assumes 5-ounce cups, the most common standard. If your machine uses 6-ounce cups, add about 20% more coffee to every number below.

In practice, a full 12-cup pot fills about four to five ordinary 12-ounce mugs, not twelve.

The Ratio Chart

These numbers use a 1:16 ratio: 1 gram of coffee for every 16 grams (or milliliters) of water. Tablespoons assume about 5 g per level tablespoon of medium-ground coffee. A standard coffee scoop holds 2 tablespoons, about 10 g.

4 cups 20 oz / 590 ml 37–40 g 7–8 4
6 cups 30 oz / 890 ml 55 g 11 5½

For the 4-cup row, the chart rounds the coffee up slightly on purpose. That's explained in the section on small batches below.

Adjusting Strength for a Full Pot

Ratio is a matter of taste, not a fixed rule. Here's what a full 60-ounce pot looks like at different strengths:

  • Mild (1:18): about 99 g, or 20 tablespoons. Light, easy to drink all morning, and good for bright, fruity light roasts.
  • Balanced (1:17): about 104 g, or 21 tablespoons. A good middle ground.
  • Standard (1:16): about 111 g, or 22 tablespoons. A good starting point for most people.
  • Strong (1:15): about 118 g, or 24 tablespoons. Rich and full-bodied, and it holds up well to milk.

Many coffee cans suggest "1 tablespoon per cup." For a 12-cup pot that's only about 60 g of coffee, roughly half of what's needed, which is why so much home drip coffee tastes thin. If your coffee has always seemed weak, this is probably the cause.

Why Weighing Beats Scooping

Tablespoons work, but they aren't precise. A heaping scoop can weigh twice as much as a level one. Dark roasts are less dense than light roasts, so the same scoop holds less coffee by weight. Finely ground coffee also packs tighter than coarse.

A basic digital kitchen scale that reads in grams removes all of that guesswork, and it doesn't need to be expensive. Put your filter basket on the scale, tare it, and add coffee until you reach your number. It takes about ten seconds, and your coffee will taste the same from day to day.

If you don't want a scale, you can still be consistent. Use the same scoop every time, level it off with a knife, and keep track of how many you use. That's enough for most people.

How to Brew a Great 12-Cup Pot

  1. Measure your water with the carafe. Fill the carafe to the 12 line and pour it into the reservoir. Use cold, filtered water if your tap water tastes of chlorine or minerals, since coffee is about 98% water.
  2. Rinse your paper filter. Run some hot water through the filter before adding coffee. This removes the papery taste and warms the basket. Use the filter shape your machine calls for. Most 12-cup machines take either flat-bottom basket filters or #4 cone filters. Good unbleached basket coffee filters are inexpensive and work well.
  3. Grind medium. For drip machines, aim for a texture like coarse sand or kosher salt. If the grind is too fine, water backs up in the basket and the coffee turns bitter. If it's too coarse, water rushes through and the coffee tastes sour and weak.
  4. Weigh or scoop your coffee and shake the basket gently to level the bed. An even bed helps the water extract evenly.
  5. Brew, then stir. Coffee that drips out first is stronger than coffee that drips out last. Give the carafe a quick stir before you pour the first cup.
  6. Get it off the hot plate. After about 20–30 minutes on a warming plate, coffee starts to taste stewed and bitter. If you won't finish the pot quickly, pour it into a thermos.

Small Batches Need a Slightly Stronger Ratio

Most drip machines are designed to brew a full pot. When you brew only 4 cups in a 12-cup machine, the coffee bed in the basket is shallow, and water passes through it faster than it should. The result is often a little weak or sour.

The fix is easy. For 4–6 cups, use a ratio of about 1:15 (the chart's 4-cup row already does this). Some machines have a "1–4 cup" or "small batch" setting that slows the brew. If yours does, use it.

If you regularly brew only a few cups, a smaller brewer may suit you better. A pour-over cone or a 5-cup machine handles small volumes better.

Watch Your Basket Capacity

A full 12-cup dose is a lot of coffee, and it swells when it gets wet. Some smaller baskets overflow when you go above about 110–120 g, especially with very fresh coffee, which releases more gas and foams more as it brews. If you see grounds in your cup or water spilling over the basket edge:

  • Grind slightly coarser so water drains faster.
  • Make sure the filter is seated properly and not folded over.
  • Cut back to about 105 g and use a slightly darker roast for more body.

Getting the Most From Your Beans

The ratio matters, but freshness matters just as much. Whole beans ground just before brewing taste much better than coffee ground weeks ago. If you're ready to upgrade, a burr coffee grinder gives a more even grind than a blade grinder, and that makes a real difference in a drip machine.

You don't need one to make good coffee, though. Pre-ground coffee at the right ratio is far better than freshly ground coffee at the wrong ratio. Get your measurements right first, then upgrade your equipment if you want to.

FAQ

How many scoops of coffee for a 12-cup coffee maker? About 11 level scoops, using a standard 2-tablespoon coffee scoop. For stronger coffee, use 12. For milder coffee, use 10. Level each scoop, because heaping scoops add a lot of extra coffee over 11 scoops.

Why does my coffee taste bitter even though I'm using the right amount? Bitterness usually comes from over-extraction. The common causes are a grind that's too fine, coffee left on the warming plate too long, or a machine that needs descaling. Mineral buildup slows the water flow and changes the brewing temperature. Try a coarser grind and move the coffee to a thermos once it's brewed. If you haven't cleaned the machine in a few months, run a descaling cycle with vinegar or descaler.

Can I make half a pot without changing the ratio? Mostly, yes. Halve the coffee and the water. For 6 cups, that's about 55 g, or 11 tablespoons. Because drip machines extract less efficiently with smaller amounts, adding an extra tablespoon or two often helps half-pots taste as good as a full one.
