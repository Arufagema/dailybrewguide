Best Cheap Scale with Timer for Pour-Over Coffee

2026-10-21

If you just want the answer: buy a basic digital coffee scale with a built-in timer that reads in 0.1 g steps, holds at least 2 kg, and responds in under a second. You don't need Bluetooth, an app, or automatic flow-rate tracking to make excellent pour-over. A plain coffee scale with timer 0.1g in the budget range covers everything a V60, Kalita, or Chemex brewer actually needs.

The rest of this guide explains what to look for, what to skip, and how to tell in five minutes whether the scale you bought is good enough.

Why a Scale With a Timer Matters for Pour-Over

Pour-over is unforgiving. Small changes in dose, water amount, and timing show up right away in the cup. A scoop of coffee can vary by 20% or more depending on the roast and grind. Eyeballing water from a kettle is even less reliable.

A scale with a timer lets you control the three variables that matter most:

  • Dose: how much coffee goes in (for example, 15 g)
  • Water: how much water goes in, and when (for example, 250 g total)
  • Time: how long the bloom and the whole brew take (for example, a 2:45–3:30 drawdown)

When the scale and timer are in one unit, you can watch both while you pour without juggling your phone. That convenience is most of what you're paying for.

What Actually Matters in a Cheap Scale

1. Resolution: 0.1 g is the sweet spot

Some kitchen scales only read in 1 g steps. That's workable for water, but on a 15 g dose a 1 g error is almost 7%. Look for 0.1 g resolution. You don't need 0.01 g. That level of precision belongs to espresso nerds and jewelers.

2. Response time: the spec nobody lists

This is where cheap scales fall apart the most. A slow scale lags behind your pour, so you overshoot your target and then watch the number keep climbing. You want the display to keep up with a steady stream of water, ideally within about half a second.

Sellers rarely list this, so check it yourself (see the test below) and return the scale if it lags badly.

3. Capacity: at least 2 kg

Your brewer, a carafe or mug, coffee, and water can easily weigh 1–1.5 kg, and a Chemex with a big batch weighs more. A 2 kg capacity gives you headroom. Many tiny "pocket" scales top out at 500 g, which is too low for pour-over.

4. A timer that's easy to start

The timer should start and stop with one button press, ideally one you can hit without looking. Some budget scales start the timer automatically when they sense weight. That's a nice touch but not essential.

5. Platform size and display placement

The platform should be at least about 12 × 12 cm so a carafe sits flat and stable. Make sure the display isn't hidden under your brewer. Wide drippers like a Chemex or a flat-bottom brewer can block a display set too far back on a small platform.

6. Auto-off you can disable (or that's long enough)

Some cheap scales shut off after 60 seconds of no change. That's a problem if you pause to let the bloom settle. Look for an auto-off of at least 3–5 minutes, or one you can turn off.

7. Power: rechargeable vs. batteries

USB-C rechargeable scales are convenient, and you'll charge one every few weeks with daily use. Coin-cell (CR2032) or AAA models are fine too. Just keep spares around. Neither is clearly better. Pick whichever you're less likely to forget about.

What You Can Safely Skip

  • Bluetooth and apps. Flow-rate graphs are fun, but they won't fix a bad grind or bad technique. Most people stop opening the app within a month.
  • Water resistance claims. A few drips won't hurt most scales. Wipe them off and don't submerge the scale.
  • Built-in recipe modes. Your recipe fits on a sticky note.

The Five-Minute Test for Any New Scale

Run these checks during the return window:

  1. Accuracy check: Weigh a few coins of a known mass (look up the official weights for your country's coins), or a sealed 500 g bag of something. It should be within about 1–2 g on the 500 g item and within 0.1–0.2 g on small objects.
  2. Drift check: Put a mug on the scale and tare it. Wait 60 seconds. It should still read 0.0, or at most bounce by 0.1 g.
  3. Lag check: Pour water slowly and steadily into a cup on the scale, then stop abruptly. The number should settle within about a second. If it keeps climbing by 3–5 g after you stop, it's too slow for pour-over.
  4. Heat check: Brew a full cup. A hot carafe sitting on cheap scales can cause slight drift. Watch whether the reading wanders as the brew finishes.
  5. Auto-off check: Leave it alone with weight on it and time how long it stays on.

If it passes all five, keep it. Spending more won't change how your coffee tastes.

A Simple Pour-Over Recipe to Use With Your Scale

This works well on a cone dripper with a medium-fine grind:

  • Coffee: 15 g
  • Water: 250 g at roughly 92–96 °C (just off the boil)
  • Ratio: 1:16.7

Steps:

  1. Rinse the paper filter, discard the rinse water, add the coffee, and tare the scale.
  2. Start the timer and pour 45 g of water for the bloom. Swirl gently.
  3. At 0:45, pour slowly up to 150 g.
  4. At 1:15, pour up to 250 g.
  5. Let it drain. Aim to finish around 2:45–3:30.

Adjust from there:

  • Finishes too fast, tastes sour or thin? Grind finer.
  • Finishes too slow, tastes bitter or harsh? Grind coarser.
  • Tastes good but weak? Use 16 g of coffee and keep the water the same.

That loop of adjusting one variable at a time is the real reason to own a scale. Without numbers, you're guessing.

Pairing It With the Right Kit

A scale makes the biggest difference when your pour is controllable. If you're still pouring from a regular kettle, a gooseneck kettle is the natural next upgrade. It makes hitting those 45 g / 150 g / 250 g marks much easier. Grind consistency matters even more than either, so if your budget allows only one more purchase after the scale, consider a burr coffee grinder before anything else.

FAQ

Can I just use my kitchen scale and my phone's timer?

Yes, if your kitchen scale reads in 1 g steps and responds quickly. It's a perfectly fine way to start. The two main downsides are the coarser dose accuracy and juggling two devices mid-pour. If you find yourself brewing pour-over most days, a dedicated coffee scale is a small, worthwhile upgrade.

Should I put a mat or pad under the brewer to protect the scale?

A thin silicone pad helps with heat and spills, and many coffee scales come with one. Tare the scale after placing the pad and brewer so the pad's weight doesn't count. Avoid thick, squishy mats, which can make readings sluggish.

How long do cheap coffee scales last?

With reasonable care, many last years. They usually fail from water getting into the buttons or the charging port, or from load cells damaged by drops or overloading. Wipe up spills promptly, never exceed the rated capacity, and don't store heavy things on top of the scale.
