How to Dial In a Pour Over: A Beginner Ratio Guide

2026-10-09

Start here: for most pour overs, use a 1:16 ratio — 1 gram of coffee for every 16 grams of water. So 20g of coffee needs about 320g of water, which yields roughly a 10–11 oz cup. Grind at a medium texture (like table salt), aim for a total brew time of 2:30 to 3:30, and keep your water just off the boil at around 200–205°F (93–96°C). If you do only that, you'll make good coffee today.

Everything below is how to go from "good" to "dialed in for your beans and your taste."

The one number that matters most: your ratio

Ratio is just coffee weight to water weight. It's the single biggest lever you have, and it's why a kitchen scale beats scoops every time. Scoops measure volume, and coffee beans vary wildly in density — a light roast and a dark roast scooped identically can differ by several grams.

Here's a practical starting grid:

Strength Ratio Example (coffee → water)
Bold, punchy 1:15 20g → 300g
Balanced (start here) 1:16 20g → 320g
Light, tea-like 1:17 20g → 340g

If you're brand new, start at 1:16 and only move once you've tasted it. Most people can taste the difference between 1:15 and 1:17 clearly, so these are meaningful steps, not hair-splitting.

A kitchen scale with timer that reads to 0.1g is the one upgrade I'd insist on before anything else. It turns "I think this tastes better" into something you can actually repeat tomorrow.

Grind size: the dial you'll touch most

Once your ratio is set, grind size is how you fine-tune. It controls extraction — how much flavor the water pulls from the coffee.

  • Too coarse → water rushes through → sour, thin, watery, weak. Brew finishes fast (under ~2:00).
  • Too fine → water chokes and stalls → bitter, harsh, drying. Brew drags on (past ~4:00).
  • Just right → sweet, balanced, clear. Brew lands in your target time window.

Start at a medium grind, roughly the texture of table salt. Then taste and adjust one notch at a time:

  • Coffee tastes sour or sharp? Grind finer.
  • Coffee tastes bitter or hollow? Grind coarser.

A note worth the money: a quality burr grinder matters more than a fancy brewer. Blade grinders chop beans into uneven chunks, which means some grounds over-extract while others under-extract in the same cup — you literally can't dial that in. A consistent conical burr coffee grinder is the difference between chasing your tail and actually converging on a recipe.

Your first dial-in, step by step

Let's brew one cup at 1:16 so you have a concrete reference point. This assumes a cone-style dripper and a paper filter.

What you'll need

  • 20g coffee, freshly ground medium
  • 320g water at ~200–205°F
  • A scale, a filter, and your dripper over a mug or carafe

A gooseneck kettle makes this dramatically easier — the narrow spout lets you pour slowly and exactly where you want, which is most of the "technique" in pour over.

The steps

  1. Rinse the filter. Pour hot water through the empty paper filter into your mug, then dump that water. This removes papery taste and warms everything up.
  2. Add coffee and zero the scale. Give the grounds a gentle shake to level them.
  3. Bloom (0:00–0:45). Pour 2x the coffee weight in water — for 20g, that's 40g — just enough to wet all the grounds. Fresh coffee will puff up and release CO₂. Let it sit 30–45 seconds. This degassing step makes later extraction more even.
  4. Pour in stages (0:45–~2:00). Add water in slow, steady spirals, starting in the center and working outward, staying away from the very edge. Pour in 2–3 additions, letting the water level drop a bit between each, until you hit 320g total.
  5. Let it draw down. The bed should finish dripping around 2:30–3:30 total. When it's done, the spent grounds should look flat and even, not cratered or heavily mounded on one side.
  6. Swirl, then taste. Give the mug a gentle swirl and taste it black first — milk and sugar hide the signals you're trying to read.

Reading the result

What you taste What it means Next change
Sour, salty, sharp Under-extracted Grind finer
Bitter, dry, harsh Over-extracted Grind coarser
Flat, weak, thin Too little coffee Lower ratio (try 1:15)
Too intense, heavy Too much coffee Raise ratio (try 1:17)

Change one variable at a time and keep brief notes. Two or three brews is usually all it takes to land somewhere you genuinely like.

Dialing in for different roasts

Light roasts are denser and harder to extract, so they generally like a finer grind, hotter water (closer to 205°F), and sometimes a slightly longer contact time. Dark roasts are more soluble and more fragile — give them a slightly coarser grind and cooler water (around 195–200°F) to avoid pulling out ashy bitterness.

If you switch beans and your reliable recipe suddenly tastes off, that's expected. Reset to 1:16 at a medium grind and re-dial from there.

A few honest tradeoffs

  • Freshness beats gear. Coffee roasted within the last 2–4 weeks and ground right before brewing will outperform expensive equipment used with stale, pre-ground coffee. If you buy one thing, buy fresh beans.
  • Chasing numbers has limits. Water temperature, pour speed, and even your local water's mineral content all nudge the result. Don't obsess — ratio and grind do 80% of the work.
  • "Correct" is what you like. These targets are starting points backed by how extraction behaves, not laws. If you prefer a bolder, slightly over-extracted cup, that's a valid preference, not a mistake.

FAQ

Do I really need a scale, or can I use scoops? You really want a scale. Bean density varies enough that a "scoop" is an unreliable measure, and without consistent input you can't tell whether a change came from your recipe or just from measuring differently. A basic scale removes that guesswork for about the cost of a few bags of beans.

My coffee tastes both sour and bitter at the same time. What's happening? That usually points to an uneven grind — some particles over-extracting while others under-extract — which is the classic signature of a blade grinder or very inconsistent burrs. Tightening up your grind consistency fixes the "muddy" taste more than any ratio tweak will.

How much does water temperature actually matter? Less than ratio and grind, but it's real. Off-boil water (200–205°F) is a safe default. Cooler water mutes acidity and body; hotter water extracts more aggressively. If you don't own a thermometer, just let a boiling kettle rest for about 30–45 seconds before pouring — that lands you in the right range.
